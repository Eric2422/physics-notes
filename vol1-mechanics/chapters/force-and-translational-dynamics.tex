\section{Center of Mass}

The \textbf{center of mass} (\textbf{c.m.}) allows us to simplify
many physical calculations.
In many cases, objects can be treated as point masses at their center of mass.

Assuming that there is gravity,
there are two ways to experimentally determine an object's center of mass:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Balancing the object, e.g., on your finger or penciltip.
    \item Plumb lines
        (in AP Physics C:~Mechanics, I did this with
        irregularly-shaped cardboard cutouts)
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Hang the object from any point, allowing it to hang freely.
            \item Using a plumb bob (string with a weight),
                find the line directly down from the point of attachment.
            \item Mark this line on the object.
            \item Repeat steps 1 and 3 multiple times with different points.
            \item Find where the lines intersect.
                This should be the center of mass.
        \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

Determing the c.m. theoretically
depends on whether the mass distribution is \textbf{discrete} or
\textbf{continuous}.

\begin{subequations}
    For discrete masses (e.g., several balls), the center of mass is
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            x_{\mathrm{c.m.}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i x_i}{\sum_i m_i} \\
            y_{\mathrm{c.m.}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i y_i}{\sum_i m_i} \\
            z_{\mathrm{c.m.}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i z_i}{\sum_i m_i}
        \end{aligned}.
    \end{equation}
    More compactly, we can re-write it as the vector
    \begin{equation}
        \vecb{r_{\mathrm{c.m.}}} = \frac{\sum_i m_i \vecb{r}_i}{m_i}.
    \end{equation}
\end{subequations}

For a continuous mass distribution, the c.m. is given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:continuous-cm}
    \vecb{r_{\mathrm{c.m.}}} = \frac{\int \vecb{r} \odif{m}}{\int \odif{m}},
\end{equation}
where \(\odif{m}\) is a small amount of mass.

Integrating with respect to mass is difficult,
so let us find a way to make mass dependent on something easier,
e.g., distance.

\subsection{Linear Mass Density}

\textbf{Linear mass density} is mass per length,
e.g., kilograms per metre \unit{\kilogram \per \metre}.
Thus, the formula is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \frac{m}{\ell}.
\end{equation}

Instantaneous linear mass density as a function of distance is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(r) = \odv{m}{r},
\end{equation}
which we can arrange into
\begin{equation}
    \odif{m} = \lambda(r) \odif{r}.
\end{equation}
Plugging back into \pgRef{eq:continuous-cm}, the center of mass is
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{c.m.}} =
    \frac{\int r \lambda(r) \odif{r}}{\int\lambda(r) \odif{r}}.
\end{equation}
With this equation,
we can find the c.m. as long as the linear mass density is integrable.

\subsection{Area and Volume Mass Density}

For higher dimensions, area and volume mass density exist,
with symbols of \(\sigma\) and \(\rho\)
and SI units of \unit{\kilogram \per \metre\squared}
and \unit{\kilogram \per \metre\cubed}, respectively.

They allow us to find the c.m. in higher dimensions,
However, this requires multivariable calculus,
so AP Physics C:~Mechanics does not ask us to do this.

For completeness, it will be included.
Skip to \pgRef{sec:free-body-diagrams} if you are uninterested
or unfamiliar with multivariable calculus.

The concept itself is not that difficult actually.
You just compress the higher dimension density functions
down into a linear density function using integration.

For an area density,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_\mathrm{c.m.} &=
        \frac{\iint_A x \sigma(x, y, z) \odif{A}}
        {\iint_A \sigma(x, y) \odif{A}}, \\
        y_\mathrm{c.m.} &=
        \frac{\iint_A y \sigma(x, y, z) \odif{A}}
        {\iint_A \sigma(x, y) \odif{A}}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For a volume density,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_\mathrm{c.m.} &=
        \frac{\iiint_V x \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}
        {\iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}, \\
        y_\mathrm{c.m.} &=
        \frac{\iiint_V y \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}
        {\iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}, \\
        z_\mathrm{c.m.} &=
        \frac{\iiint_V z \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}
        {\iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Gravity}

The \textbf{gravitational force} outside of a sphere is given by
\textbf{Newton's law of gravity},
\begin{equation} \label{eq:newtons-law-of-gravity}
    F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F\) is the magnitude of the gravitational force,
    \item \(G = \qty{6.674 30(15)e-11}{\metre\cubed \per \kilogram \per
        \second\squared}\)
        is the universal gravitational constant, \autocite{nist-G}
    \item \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are the masses of the two objects,
    \item \(r\) is the distance between the two.
\end{itemize}

The \textbf{gravitational field} \(g\) outside of a sphere is
\begin{equation}
    g = G \frac{m}{r^2},
\end{equation}
where \(m\) is the mass of the sphere.

For the gravitational field \emph{inside} a sphere,
if \(r'\) is a dummy integration variable for radius from the center,
\(\rho(r')\) is the density as a function of \(r'\),
and \(r\) is the radius of an imaginary inner sphere,
the magnitude of the gravitational field at distance \(r\)
from the center is
\begin{equation}
    g = \frac{G}{r^2}
    \int_{0}^{r} 4\pi (r')^2 \rho(r') \odif{r'}.
\end{equation}
The gravity at all points inside a hollow spherical shell is 0,
so the mass beyond radius \(r'\) can be ignored.

For a sphere of constant density \(\rho\), mass \(M\),
and radius \(R\),
when the distance from the center \(r\) is less than \(R\) (\(r < R\)),
the gravitational field \(\vecb{g}\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        g &= \frac{G}{r^2} \int_{0}^{r} 4\pi
        (r')^2 \rho \odif{r'} \\
        &= \frac{4 G \pi \rho}{r^2}
        \int_{0}^{R} (r')^2 \odif{r'} \\
        &= \frac{4 G \pi \rho}{r^2} \frac{r^3}{3} \\
        &= \frac{4}{3} \pi G \rho r \\
        &= \frac{4}{3} \pi G \frac{M}{\frac{4}{3} \pi R^3} r \\
        &= \frac{G M r}{R^3}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Resistive Forces}

\textbf{Resistive forces} act opposite to the direction of movement.

\subsection{Friction}

Types of \textbf{friction}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \textbf{Kinetic friction} is between two surfaces with
        non-zero relative velocity.
        It is constant and independent of the object's velocity.
    \item
        \textbf{Static friction} is between two surfaces with zero
        relative velocity
        and increases as the force attempting to move the object increases.
\end{itemize}

The magnitude of kinetic friction \(F_\mathrm{fk}\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{fk} = \mu_\mathrm{k} F_\mathrm{N},
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_\mathrm{k}\) is the coefficient of kinetic friction
and \(F_\mathrm{N}\) is the normal force.

The magnitude of static friction \(F_\mathrm{fs}\) is
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{fs} \le \mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N},
\end{equation}
where \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\) is the coefficient of static friction
and \(F_\mathrm{N}\) is the normal force.

In all cases,
\begin{equation}
    \mu_\mathrm{k} < \mu_\mathrm{s}.
\end{equation}
In other words, kinetic friction is always less than the maximum
static friction:
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{fk} < \mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Drag}

\textbf{Drag} is a force that acts opposite to the movement of a body in fluid.
The proper formula for the magnitude of drag is
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{D} = \frac{1}{2} \rho v^2 C_\mathrm{D} A,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F_\mathrm{D}\) is the magnitude of the drag force;
    \item \(A\) is the frontal area;
    \item \(\rho\) is the fluid density;
    \item \(v\) is the relative velocity between the object and fluid;
    \item \(C_\mathrm{D}\) is an experimentally-determined drag coefficient,
        which is influenced by factors such as material, shape, etc.
\end{itemize}
However, this results in a differential equations problem
that is too mathematically complicated for AP Physics C:~Mechanics.

A simpler approximation for the drag \(\vecb{F}_\mathrm{D}\) is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear-drag}
    \vecb{F_\mathrm{D}} = - b \vecb{v},
\end{equation}
where \(b\) is an experimentally-determined constant
and \(\vecb{v}\) is the relative velocity between the object and fluid.

\textbf{Terminal velocity} is when the forces of gravity and drag
balance each other out.
Therefore, acceleration is 0 at terminal velocity.
If an object is fallling vertically, with up as positive,
the terminal velocity \(v_\mathrm{t}\) is achieved at
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear-terminal-velocity}
    \begin{aligned}
        - b v_\mathrm{t} - m g &= 0 \\
        - b v_\mathrm{t} &= m g \\
        v_\mathrm{t} &= - \frac{m}{b} g. \\
    \end{aligned} \\
\end{equation}

Intuitively, we know that a falling object must approach terminal velocity.
Thus, we can conjecture that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:terminal-velocity-limit}
    \lim_{t \to \infty} v = v_\mathrm{t}.
\end{equation}

Now that we know the terminal velocity of a falling object,
let us derive its velocity as a function of time.
\footnote{
    I do not have the derivations
    for the position and velocity of fallling objects with linear
    drag in my notes,
    so I do not remember the exact process taught in class.
    The following has been solved
    using a technique I learned from a later differential equations class,
    so it probably differs from what was taught in AP Physics C:~Mechanics.
}
We start with Newton's second law of motion [\pgRef{eq:newtons-2nd-law}]
and \pgRef{eq:linear-drag},
finding that
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{gather} \label{eq:drag-diff-eq}
        \begin{aligned}
            F &= m a \\
            - m g - bv &= m a \\
            - m g - bv &= m \odv{v}{t} \\
            \odv{v}{t} + \frac{b}{m} v &= - g \\
            \odv{v}{t} e^{\frac{b}{m} t} + \frac{b}{m} e^{\frac{b}{m}
            t} v &= - g e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \\
            \odv{}{t} \left[v e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \right] &= - g
            e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \\
            v e^{\frac{b}{m} t} &= \int - g e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \odif{t} \\
            v e^{\frac{b}{m} t} &= - \frac{m}{b} g e^{\frac{b}{m} t} + C \\
            v &= - \frac{m}{b} g + C e^{- \frac{b}{m} t}. \\
        \end{aligned}
    \end{gather}
    Solving for \(C\) using the initial condition \(v(0) = v_0\), we obtain
    \begin{gather}
        \begin{aligned}
            v_0 &= - \frac{m}{b} g + C e^{- \frac{b}{m} 0} \\
            v_0 &= - \frac{m}{b} g + C \\
            C &= v_0 + \frac{m}{b} g.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{gather}
    Therefore, by plugging \(C\) back into \pgRef{eq:drag-diff-eq},
    the velocity is
    \begin{equation}
        v = - \frac{m}{b} g + \ab(v_0 + \frac{m}{b} g) e^{- \frac{b}{m} t}.
    \end{equation}
    Using terminal velocity as given in \pgRef{eq:linear-terminal-velocity},
    the velocity of a falling body with linear drag is
    \begin{equation}
        v = v_\mathrm{t} + \ab(v_0 - v_\mathrm{t}) e^{- \frac{b}{m} t}.
    \end{equation}
\end{subequations}
As \(t\) approaches infinity, the exponential component approaches 0.
Therefore, the limit is
\begin{equation*}
    \lim_{t \to \infty} v = v_\mathrm{t}.
\end{equation*}
This agrees with our intuition in \pgRef{eq:terminal-velocity-limit}.

Integrating again to find position, we find
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \odv{y}{t} &= v_\mathrm{t} + \ab(v_0 - v_\mathrm{t}) e^{-
        \frac{b}{m} t} \\
        y &= \int v_\mathrm{t} + \ab(v_0 - v_\mathrm{t}) e^{-
        \frac{b}{m} t} \odif{t} \\
        y &= y_0
        + v_\mathrm{t} t
        + \ab(v_\mathrm{t} - v_0)
        \frac{m}{b}
        e^{- \frac{b}{m} t},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(y_0\) is the initial height.

\section{Springs}

\textbf{Ideal springs} are springs that perfectly follow \textbf{Hooke's law},
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hookes-law}
    F_\mathrm{s} = - k \Delta{x},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F_\mathrm{s}\) is the magnitude of the spring force;
    \item \(k\) is the \textbf{spring constant},
        essentially a measure of how stiff the spring is,
        with SI units of \unit{\newton \per \metre};
    \item \(\Delta{x}\) is the displacement of the end of the spring
        from its rest position.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multiple Springs}

Springs can be connected in \textbf{series} or \textbf{parallel}.
In either case, they act like a single spring.

Springs in series are connected side by side.
Each spring experiences the same force.
The net spring constant \(k_\mathrm{net}\)is given by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{k_\mathrm{net}} = \sum_i \frac{1}{k_i}.
\end{equation}
In practice,
this means that springs connected in series are equivalent to a
single weaker spring.

Springs in parallel are connected end to end.
Each spring stretches the same distance.
The net spring constant is given by
\begin{equation}
    k_\mathrm{net} = \sum_i k_i.
\end{equation}
In practice,
this means that springs connected in parallel are equivalent to a
single stronger spring.

\section{Circular Motion}

\textbf{Centripetal acceleration} is "center-seeking" acceleration.

Moving in a circle with constant speed is \textbf{uniform circular motion}.
Its velocity \(v\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    v = \frac{2 \pi r}{T},
\end{equation}
where \(r\) is the radius and \(T\) is the revolution period.
Its centripetal acceleration \(a_\mathrm{c}\) is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:centripetal-acceleration}
    a_\mathrm{c} = \frac{2 \pi v}{T} = \frac{4 \pi ^2 r}{T^2} = \frac{v^2}{r}.
\end{equation}

The revolution period \(T\) is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:revolution-period}
    T = \frac{2 \pi r}{v},
\end{equation}
where \(r\) is the revolution radius and \(v\) is the tangential velocity.

The \textbf{centripetal force} \(F_\mathrm{c}\) is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:centripetal-force}
    F_\mathrm{c} = m \frac{v^2}{r}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Critical velocity} is the minimum velocity need for circular motion.
To find the critical velocity for a given centripetal force \(F\),
use \pgRef{eq:centripetal-force}.
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        m \frac{v^2}{r} &= F \\
        v &= \sqrt{\frac{F r}{m}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Orbital Velocity} \label{subsec:orbital-velocity}

Let \(m\) and \(M\) be the masses of two bodies such that \(m \ll M\).
Combing Newton's law of gravity,
\pgRef{eq:newtons-law-of-gravity},
with \pgRef{eq:centripetal-force}, we find
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        G \frac{M m}{r^2} &= m \frac{v^2}{r} \\
        G \frac{M}{r} &= v^2 \\
        v &= \sqrt{G \frac{M}{r}}
    \end{aligned}.
\end{equation}
Using \pgRef{eq:revolution-period}, we find the period \(T\) is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{2 \pi r}{T} &= \sqrt{G \frac{M}{r}} \\
        \frac{T}{2 \pi r} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{G \frac{M}{r}}} \\
        &= \sqrt{\frac{r}{G M}} \\
        T &= \frac{2 \pi r^{3/2}}{\sqrt{G M}} \\
        T^2 &= \frac{4 \pi^2}{G M} r^3 \\
    \end{aligned}.
\end{equation}

This derivation leads us to \textbf{Kepler's third law of planetary motion}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kepler-3rd-law}
    T^2 \propto r^3.
\end{equation}
For more details on planetary motion, see \pgRef{sec:orbits}.
