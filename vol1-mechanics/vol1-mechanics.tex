\section{Newton's Laws of Motion}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \label{itm:newtons-1st-law}
        An stationary object stays at rest,
        and a moving object continues moving with constant velocity,
        until acted upon by an external force.
    \item
        \begin{equation} \label{eq:newtons-2nd-law}
            \bm{F} = m\bm{a}
        \end{equation}
        where
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \(\bm{F}\) is the the force exerted on the object.
            \item \(m\) is the mass of the object.
            \item \(\bm{a}\) is the acceleration of the object.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \label{itm:newtons-3rd-law}
        Each force has an equal and opposite reacting force.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Kinematic Equations}

The below \textbf{kinematic equations} are only true under constant acceleration.
\begin{gather} \label{eq:linear-kinematic}
    {
        \Delta{x} = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2
        \qquad \mathrm{or} \qquad
        x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2
    } \\
    v_\mathrm{f} = v_0 + a t \\
    v^2 = v_0^2 + 2 a \Delta{x} \\
    \Delta{x} = \frac{v_0 + v_\mathrm{f}}{2} t
\end{gather}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \(x_0\) is the initial position,
        \(x_\mathrm{f}\) is the final position,
        and \(\Delta{x}\) is the change in position.
    \item
        \(v_0\) is the initial velocity,
        and \(v_\mathrm{f}\) is the final velocity.
    \item \(a\) is the acceleration.
    \item \(t\) is the time elapsed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Center of Mass}

The \textbf{center of mass} (\textbf{CoM}) allows us to simplify many physical calculations.
In many cases, objects can be treated as point masses at their center of mass.

Assuming that there is gravity,
there are two ways to experimentally determine an object's center of mass:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Balancing the object, e.g., on your finger or penciltip.
    \item
        Plumb lines (in AP Physics C: Mechanics, I did this with irregularly-shaped cardboard cutouts)
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item Hang the object from any point, allowing it to hang freely.
            \item
                Using a plumb bob (string with a weight),
                find the line directly down from the point of attachment.
            \item Mark this line on the object.
            \item Repeat steps 1 and 3 multiple times with different points.
            \item
                Find where the lines intersect.
                This should be the center of mass.
        \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

Determing the CoM theoretically
depends on whether the mass distribution is \textbf{discrete} or \textbf{continuous}.

For discrete masses (e.g., several balls):
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_{\mathrm{cm}} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{m_i} \\
        y_{\mathrm{cm}} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{m_i} \\
        z_{\mathrm{cm}} = \frac{\sum m_i z_i}{m_i}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
More compactly,
\begin{equation}
    \bm{r_{\mathrm{cm}}} = \frac{\sum m_i \bm{r_i}}{m_i}
\end{equation}


For a continuous mass distribution:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:continuous-com}
    \bm{r}_{\mathrm{cm}} = \frac{\int \bm{r} \odif{m}}{\int \odif{m}}
\end{equation}
where \(\odif{m}\) is a small amount of mass.

Integrating with respect to mass is difficult,
so let us find a way to make mass dependent on something easier,
e.g., distance.

\subsection{Linear Mass Density}

\textbf{Linear mass density} is mass per length, e.g.,
kilograms per meter \(\unit{\kilogram \per \meter}\).
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \frac{m}{\ell}
\end{equation}

\(\therefore\) Instantaneous linear mass density as a function of distance is
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(r) = \odv{m}{r}
\end{equation}
which we can arrange into
\begin{equation}
    \odif{m} = \lambda(r) \odif{r}
\end{equation}

Plugging back into \cref{eq:continuous-com} (\cpageref{eq:continuous-com}):
\begin{equation}
    r_{\mathrm{cm}} = \frac{\int r \lambda(r) \odif{r}}{\int \lambda(r) \odif{r}}
\end{equation}
With this equation,
we can find the CoM as long as the linear mass density is integrable.

\subsection{Area and Volume Mass Density}

For higher dimensions, area and volume mass density exist,
with symbols of \(\sigma\) and \(\rho\)
and SI units of \unit{\kilogram \per \metre \squared}
and \unit{\kilogram \per \metre \cubed}, respectively.

They allow us to find the CoM in higher dimensions,
However, this requires multivariable calculus,
so AP Physics C: Mechanics does not ask us to do this.

For completeness, it will be included.
Skip to \nameRef{sec:free-body-diagrams} if you are uninterested
or unfamiliar with multivariable calculus.

The concept itself is not that difficult actually.
You just compress the higher dimension density functions
down into a linear density function using integration.

For an area density:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_\mathrm{cm} &=
            \frac{\iiint_V x \rho(x, y, z) \odif{A}}
            {\iiint_V \rho(x, y) \odif{V}} \\
        y_\mathrm{cm} &=
            \frac{\iiint_V y \rho(x, y, z) \odif{A}}
            {\iiint_V \rho(x, y) \odif{V}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For a volume density:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        x_\mathrm{cm} &=
            \frac{\iiint_V x \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}
            {\iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}} \\
        y_\mathrm{cm} &=
            \frac{\iiint_V y \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}
            {\iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}} \\
        z_\mathrm{cm} &=
            \frac{\iiint_V z \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}}
            {\iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) \odif{V}} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Free-Body Diagrams} \label{sec:free-body-diagrams}

Free-body diagrams are a simple means of illustrating the forces acting upon a body.
Draw the body as a simple shape such as a dot or box,
then add labeled arrows to represent the forces acting in each direction.
Larger arrows indicate larger forces.

\section{Gravity}

\textbf{Gravitational force} outside of a sphere is given by \textbf{Newton's law of gravity}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:newtons-law-of-gravity}
    F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F\) is the magnitude of the gravitational force.
    \item
        \(G = \qty{6.674 30(15)e-11}{\metre \cubed \per \kilogram \per \second \squared}\)
        is the universal gravitational constant. \autocite{nist-G}
    \item \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) are the masses of the two objects.
    \item \(r\) is the distance between the two.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Gravitational field} outside of a sphere:
\begin{equation}
    g = G \frac{m}{r^2}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(g\) is the gravitational force.
    \item \(m\) is the mass of the sphere.
\end{itemize}

For the gravitational field \emph{inside} a sphere,
if \(r\) is the distance from the center,
\(\rho(r)\) is the density as a function of \(r\),
and \(R_\mathrm{i}\) is the radius of an imaginary inner sphere,
the magnitude of the gravitational field at \(R_\mathrm{i}\) distance from the center is
\begin{equation}
        g = \frac{G}{R_\mathrm{i}^2} \int_{0}^{R_\mathrm{i}} 4\pi r^2 \rho(r) \odif{r}
\end{equation}

For a sphere of constant density \(\rho\), mass \(M\), and radius \(R\),
the gravitational field \(\bm{g}\) increases linearly
as distance from the center \(R_\mathrm{i}\) increases, when \(R_\mathrm{i} < R\).
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        g &= \frac{G}{R_\mathrm{i}^2} \int_{0}^{R_\mathrm{i}} 4\pi r^2 \rho \odif{r} \\
        &= \frac{4 G \pi \rho}{R_\mathrm{i}^2} \int_{0}^{R_\mathrm{i}} r^2 \odif{r} \\
        &= \frac{4 G \pi \rho}{R_\mathrm{i}^2} \frac{R_\mathrm{i}^3}{3} \\
        &= \frac{4}{3} \pi G \rho R_\mathrm{i}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Resistive Forces}

\textbf{Resistive forces} act opposite to the direction of movement.

\subsection{Friction}

Types of \textbf{friction}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \textbf{Kinetic friction} is between two surfaces with non-zero relative velocity.
        It is constant and independent of the object's velocity.
    \item
        \textbf{Static friction} is between two surfaces with zero relative velocity
        and increases as the force attempting to move the object increases.
\end{itemize}

Kinetic friction:
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{fk} = \mu_\mathrm{k} F_\mathrm{N}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F_\mathrm{fk}\) is the magnitude of kinetic friction.
    \item \(\mu_\mathrm{k}\) is the coefficient of kinetic friction.
    \item \(F_\mathrm{N}\) is the normal force.
\end{itemize}

Static friction:
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{fs} \le \mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F_\mathrm{fs}\) is the magnitude of static friction.
    \item \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\) is the coefficient of static friction.
    \item \(F_\mathrm{N}\) is the normal force.
\end{itemize}

In all cases,
\begin{equation}
    \mu_\mathrm{k} < \mu_\mathrm{s}
\end{equation}
In other words, kinetic friction is always less than the maximum static friction:
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{fk} < \mu_\mathrm{s} F_\mathrm{N}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Drag}

\textbf{Drag} is a force that acts opposite to the movement of a body in fluid.

The proper formula for drag is
\begin{equation}
    F_\mathrm{D} = \frac{1}{2} \rho v^2 C_\mathrm{D} A
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F_\mathrm{D}\) is the magnitude of the drag force.
    \item \(A\) is the frontal area.
    \item \(\rho\) is the fluid density.
    \item \(v\) is the relative velocity between object and fluid.
    \item
        \(C_\mathrm{D}\) is an experimentally-determined drag coefficient,
        which is influenced by factors such as material, shape, etc.
\end{itemize}

However, this is way too complicated for AP Physics C: Mechanics.
The above formula is more appropriate for higher level college physics.

A simpler approximation for drag is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{F_\mathrm{D}} = -b \bm{v}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\bm{F}_\mathrm{D}\) is the drag acting upon the object.
    \item \(b\) is an experimentally-determined constant.
    \item \(v\) is the relative velocity between object and fluid.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Terminal velocity} is when the forces from gravity and drag
balance out each other.
\(\therefore\) Acceleration is 0 at terminal velocity.
With linear drag, given that up is positive, terminal velocity is achieved at
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear-terminal-velocity}
    \begin{aligned}
        bv_\mathrm{t} - mg &= 0 \\
        bv_\mathrm{t} &= mg \\
        v_\mathrm{t} &= \frac{m}{b} g \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Velocity of a free-falling object experiencing linear drag,
using Newton's second law of motion, \eqRef{eq:newtons-2nd-law}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        F &= m a\\
        mg - bv &= m a\\
        mg - bv &= m \odv{v}{t} \\
        \odv{v}{t} + \frac{b}{m} v &= g \\
        \odv{v}{t} e^{\frac{b}{m} t} + \frac{b}{m} e^{\frac{b}{m} t} v &= g e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \\
        \odv{}{t} \left[ v e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \right] &= g e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \\
        v e^{\frac{b}{m} t} &= \int g e^{\frac{b}{m} t} \odif{t} \\
        v e^{\frac{b}{m} t} &= \frac{m}{b} g e^{\frac{b}{m}t} + C \\
        v &= \frac{m}{b} g + C e^{-\frac{b}{m} t} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Solving for \(C\) using the initial condition \(v(0) = v_0\):
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \begin{aligned}
            v_0 &= \frac{m}{b} g + C e^{-\frac{b}{m} 0} \\
            v_0 &= \frac{m}{b} g + C
        \end{aligned} \\
        C = \frac{m}{b} g - v_0 \\
        v = \frac{m}{b} g + \left( \frac{m}{b} g - v_0 \right) e^{-\frac{b}{m} t}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Using terminal velocity as given in \cref{eq:linear-terminal-velocity}
(\cpageref{eq:linear-terminal-velocity}),
the velocity of a falling body with linear drag is
\begin{equation}
    v = v_\mathrm{t} + \left( v_\mathrm{t} - v_0 \right) e^{-\frac{b}{m} t}
\end{equation}

Integrate again to find position:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \odv{y}{t} &= v_\mathrm{t} + \left( v_\mathrm{t} - v_0 \right) e^{-\frac{b}{m} t} \\
        y &= \int v_\mathrm{t} + \left( v_\mathrm{t} - v_0 \right) e^{-\frac{b}{m} t} \odif{t} \\
        y &= y_0
             + v_\mathrm{t} t
             + \left( v_0 - v_\mathrm{t} \right)
               \frac{m}{b}
               e^{-\frac{b}{m} t}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Springs}

\textbf{Ideal springs} are springs that perfectly follow \textbf{Hooke's law}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hookes-law}
    F_\mathrm{s} = -k \Delta{x}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(F_\mathrm{s}\) is the magnitude of the spring force.
    \item
        \(k\) is the \textbf{spring constant},
        essentially a measure of how stiff the spring is,
        with SI units of \(\unit{\newton \per \metre}\).
    \item \(\Delta{x}\) is the displacement of the end of the spring from its rest position.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multiple Springs}

Springs can be connected in \textbf{series} or \textbf{parallel}.
In either case, they act like a single spring.

Springs in series are connected side by side.
Each spring experiences the same force.
The net spring constant is given by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{k_\mathrm{k}} = \sigma \frac{1}{k_i}
\end{equation}
In practice,
this means that springs connected in series are equivalent to a single weaker spring.

Springs in parallel are connected end to end.
Each spring stretches the same distance.
The net spring constant is given by
\begin{equation}
    k_\mathrm{net} = \sigma k_i
\end{equation}
In practice,
this means that springs connected in parallel are equivalent to a single stronger spring.

\section{Circular Motion}

\textbf{Centripetal acceleration} is "center-seeking" acceleration.

Moving in a circle with constant speed is \textbf{uniform circular motion}.
Its velocity is given by
\begin{equation}
    v = \frac{2 \pi r}{T}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(v\) is the velocity.
    \item \(r\) is the radius.
    \item \(T\) is the revolution period.
\end{itemize}
Its acceleration is given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:centripetal-acceleration}
    a = \frac{2 \pi v}{T} = \frac{4 \pi ^2 r}{T^2} = \frac{v^2}{r}
\end{equation}
where \(a\) is the acceleration.

Revolution period:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:revolution-period}
    T = \frac{2 \pi r}{v}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Centripetal force}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:centripetal-force}
    F = m \frac{v^2}{r}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Critical velocity} is the minimum velocity need for circular motion.
To find the critical velocity for a given centripetal force \(F\),
use \eqRef{eq:centripetal-force}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        m \frac{v^2}{r} &= F \\
        v &= \sqrt{\frac{F r}{m}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Orbital Velocity} \label{subsec:orbital-velocity}

Let \(m\) and \(M\) be the masses of two bodies such that \(m \ll M\).
Combining Newton's law of gravity,
\eqRef{eq:newtons-law-of-gravity},
with \eqRef{eq:centripetal-force}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        G \frac{M m}{r^2} &= m \frac{v^2}{r} \\
        G \frac{M}{r} &= v^2 \\
        v &= \sqrt{G \frac{M}{r}} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Using \eqRef{eq:revolution-period}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{2 \pi r}{T} &= \sqrt{G \frac{M}{r}} \\
        \frac{T}{2 \pi r} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{G \frac{M}{r}}} \\
        &= \sqrt{\frac{r}{G M}} \\
        T &= \frac{2 \pi r^{3/2}}{\sqrt{G M}} \\
        T^2 &= \frac{4 \pi^2}{GM} r^3 \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

This derivation leads us to \textbf{Kepler's third law of planetary motion}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:kepler-3rd-law}
    T^2 \propto r^3
\end{equation}
For more details on planetary motion, see \nameRef{sec:orbits}.

\section{Energy}

In Newtonian mechanics, kinetic energy is given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:linear-kinetic-energy}
    E_\mathrm{K} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(E_\mathrm{K}\) is the kinetic energy of the object.
    \item \(m\) is the mass of the object.
    \item \(v\) is the velocity of the object.
\end{itemize}

By definition, \textbf{conservative forces} are ones where the \textbf{work} done is \textbf{path-independent},
i.e., work along a \textbf{closed loop} is 0.

The equation for \textbf{potential energy} is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:potential-energy}
    U = - \int \bm{F} \cdot \odif{\bm{r}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Gravitational Potential Energy}

Assuming a constant gravitational field,
the difference in gravitational potential energy is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        U_\mathrm{g} &= - \int -mg \odif{y} \\
        \Delta{U_\mathrm{g}} &= mg \Delta{y}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

If the gravitational field is not constant, use Newton's law of gravity
--- \eqRef{eq:newtons-law-of-gravity}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        U_\mathrm{g} &= - \int -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \odif{r} \\
        \Delta{U_\mathrm{g}} &= -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{\Delta{r}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Elastic Potential Energy}

\textbf{Elastic potential energy} for a general spring with force function \(F(x)\):
\begin{equation} \label{eq:general-elastic-potential-energy}
    \begin{aligned}
        U_\mathrm{e} &= - \int F(x) \odif{x} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For ideal springs, we use Hooke's law, \eqRef{eq:hookes-law}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \Delta{U_\mathrm{e}} &= - \int -k x \odif{x} \\
        &= \frac{1}{2} k (\Delta{x})^2
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The derivation from \eqRef{eq:general-elastic-potential-energy}
can also be used with non-linear springs.
For example, for a spring with force function \(F(x) = -k x^\frac{4}{3}\),
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        U_\mathrm{e} &= - \int -k x^\frac{4}{3} \odif{x} \\
        \Delta{U_\mathrm{e}} &= \frac{3}{7} k x^\frac{7}{3}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\vfill

\section{Work}

Work formula:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:work}
    \begin{aligned}
        W &= \int \bm{F} \cdot \odif{\bm{x}} \\
        &= \int_{b}^{a} \bm{F}(\bm{r}(t)) \cdot \bm{r}'(t) \odif{t} \\
        &= \int_{\mathrm{C}} \bm{F} \cdot \bm{T} \odif{s}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\int_{\mathrm{C}}\) is a contour integral.
    \item \(\bm{T}\) is the tangent.
\end{itemize}

Compare the equation for work, \eqRef{eq:work},
with the equation for potential energy, \eqRef{eq:potential-energy}.
Therefore, for conservative forces,
\begin{equation}
    W = - \Delta{U}
\end{equation}

Be careful about the signs for work.
If a \emph{system} does positive work, it \emph{loses} potential energy.
Conversely, if an external force acts does positive work \emph{upon the system},
it \emph{gains} potential energy.


\section{Power}

Power is work done per unit of time.

Average power:
\begin{equation}
    P_\mathrm{avg} = \frac{W}{\Delta{t}} = \frac{\Delta{E}}{\Delta{t}}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(P_\mathrm{avg}\) is the average power.
    \item \(W\) is work done.
    \item \(\Delta{t}\) is the change in time.
    \item \(\Delta{E}\) is the change in energy.
\end{itemize}

Instantaneous power:
\begin{equation}
    P = \odv{E}{t}
\end{equation}


\chapter{Linear Momentum}

\noindent Suggested mathematical topics: \nameRef{ch:vectors}

\section{Momentum}

\textbf{Momentum} is symbolized by \(\bm{p}\):
\begin{equation}
    \bm{p} = m \bm{v}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\bm{p}\) is the momentum.
    \item \(m\) is the mass.
    \item \(\bm{v}\) is the velocity.
\end{itemize}

The SI unit of momentum is \(\unit{\kilogram \metre \per \second}\)
or \(\unit{\newton \second}\)

A system's momentum is the sum of its parts' momenta:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{p}_\mathrm{system} = \sum m_i \bm{v}_i
\end{equation}

For objects with constant mass,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta{\bm{p}} = m \Delta{\bm{v}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation} \label{eq:force-momentum}
    \begin{aligned}
        \odv{\bm{p}}{t} &= m \odv{\bm{v}}{t} \\
        \odv{\bm{p}}{t} &= m \bm{a} \\
        \bm{F} &= \odv{\bm{p}}{t}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In integral form,
\begin{equation}
    \int \bm{F}_\mathrm{net} \odif{t} = \Delta{\bm{p}}
\end{equation}

\section{Impulse}

\textbf{Impulse} is symbolized by \(\bm{J}\)
and has SI units of \(\unit{\newton \second}\) or \(\unit{\kilogram \metre \per \second}\)
In the simple case where \(\odv{\bm{F}}{t} = 0\),
\begin{equation}
    \bm{J} = \bm{F} t
\end{equation}
The more general equation is
\begin{equation}
    \int \bm{F}(t) \odif{t}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \therefore \bm{J} = \Delta{\bm{p}}
\end{equation}
where \(\Delta{\bm{p}}\) is the change in momentum.

\section{Conservation of Momentum}

Because of \eqRef{eq:force-momentum}
and \hyperref[itm:newtons-1st-law]{Newton's first law of motion}
(\cpageref{itm:newtons-1st-law}),
when there is no external force, \(\Delta{\bm{p}} = \bm{0}\),
i.e, the final and initial momentum have to be equal.

Types of \textbf{collisions}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \textbf{Elastic}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Kinetic energy is conserved.
            \item Objects bounce apart with the same energy they collided with.
        \end{itemize}
    \item
        \textbf{Inelastic}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Kinetic energy is lost.
            \item Objects bounce apart with less energy or not all.
                  The case where the objects do not bounce at all
                  is known as \textbf{perfectly inelastic}
        \end{itemize}
    \item
        \textbf{Explosion}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Kinetic energy is gained.
            \item Objects bounce apart.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Practically speaking, no collision is perfectly elastic or inelastic;
they are on a sliding continuum between the two.


\chapter{Torque and Rotational Dynamics}

\noindent Suggested mathematical topics: \nameRef{ch:vectors}

\section{Rotational Kinematics}

Rotational motion is the motion of spinning objects.
Instead of measuring distance, we measure angle.

Similarly to the linear kinematic equations
under constant linear acceleration (\cpageref{eq:linear-kinematic}),
there are \textbf{rotational kinematic equations}
under constant \textbf{angular acceleration}:
\begin{gather}
    {
        \Delta{\theta} = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2} \alpha t^2
        \qquad \mathrm{or} \qquad
        \theta_\mathrm{f} = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2} \alpha t^2
    } \\
    \omega_\mathrm{f} = \omega_0 + \alpha t \\
    \omega_\mathrm{f}^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2 \alpha \Delta{\theta} \\
    \Delta{\theta} = \frac{\omega_0 + \omega_\mathrm{f}}{2} t
\end{gather}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \(\theta_0\) is the initial angle,
        \(\theta_\mathrm{f}\) is the final angle,
        and \(\Delta{\theta}\) is the change in angle.
    \item
        \(\omega_0\) is the initial \textbf{angular velocity},
        and \(\omega_\mathrm{f}\) is the final angular velocity.
    \item \(\alpha\) is the angular acceleration.
    \item \(t\) is the time elapsed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Arc length}:
\begin{equation}
    s = r \theta
\end{equation}
where \(\theta\) is the angle in radians.
It does not work in degrees.

\textbf{Tangential velocity}:
\begin{equation}
    v_\mathrm{t} = r \omega
\end{equation}

\textbf{Tangential acceleration}:
\begin{equation}
    a_\mathrm{t} = r \alpha
\end{equation}

\subsection{Moment of Inertia}

\textbf{Moment of inertia} or \textbf{rotational inertia} is the angular analogue of mass,
describing how much a body resists rotational acceleration.

For discrete masses:
\begin{equation}
    I = \sum m_i r_i^2
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(I\) is the overall moment of inertia.
    \item \(m_i\) is the mass of each particle.
    \item \(r_i\) is the distance of each particle from the \textbf{axis of rotation}.
\end{itemize}

For continuous masses:
\begin{gather}
    I = \int r^2 \odif{m} \\
    \nonumber \\
    \begin{aligned}
        \lambda(r) &= \odv{m}{r} \\
        \therefore \odif{m} &= \lambda(r) \odif{r}
    \end{aligned} \\
    \nonumber \\
    \therefore I = \int r^2 \lambda(r) \odif{r}
\end{gather}

Rod of uniform density \(\lambda\), length \(\ell\) and mass \(M\)
rotating about an axis perpendicular to its length and passing through the center:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:uniform-rod-rotational-inertia}
    \begin{aligned}
        I &= 2 \int_{0}^{\frac{\ell}{2}} r^2 \odif{m} \\
        &= 2 \int_{0}^{\frac{\ell}{2}} r^2 \lambda \odif{r} \\
        &= 2 \lambda \int_{0}^{\frac{\ell}{2}} r^2 \odif{r} \\
        &= 2 \lambda \left[ \frac{1}{3} r^3 \right]^\frac{\ell}{2}_0 \\
        &= \frac{2 \lambda}{3} \frac{\ell^3}{8} \\
        &= \lambda \frac{\ell^3}{12} \\
        &= \frac{1}{12} M \ell^2
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

% To-do: add image

Note that the \(r\) in these formulas
means that objects have lower moment of inertia
when the axis of rotation is closer to their center of mass
than when it is far from the center of mass,
e.g., it is easier to spin a stick at the middle than at either end.
A more interesting example is that a full can rolls down a slope faster than an empty can does.
The full can's mass is more concentrated in the center,
reducing its moment of inertia and thus giving it greater acceleration.

If we already know the rotational inertia of an object
when rotating about an axis passing through its center of mass,
the \textbf{parallel axis theorem} allows us to determine the rotational inertia
when it is rotating around a new axis parallel to the original axis.
\begin{equation}
    I = I_\mathrm{cm} + m d^2
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(I\) is the new moment of inertia.
    \item
        \(I_\mathrm{cm}\) is the moment of inertia
        when rotating around the center of mass.
    \item \(m\) is the mass of the object.
    \item \(d\) is the distance between the two axes.
\end{itemize}

% To-do: add image

Using the uniform rod as an example again,
\eqRef{eq:uniform-rod-rotational-inertia},
if we rotated the rod around its end instead of its center,
its rotational inertia would be
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I &= \frac{1}{12} M \ell^2
             + M \left( \frac{\ell}{2} \right)^2 \\
        &= \frac{1}{3} M \ell^2
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Torque}

\textbf{Torque} is the rotational analogue to linear force:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \bm{\tau} &= \bm{r} \times \bm{F} \\
        \tau &= r F \sin\theta
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(\bm{\tau}\) is the torque vector, and \(\tau\) is its magnitude.
    \item \(\bm{r}\) is the position vector from the point of rotation
          to the point where the force is applied, and \(r\) is its magnitude.
    \item \(\bm{F}\) is the force applied, and \(F\) is its magnitude.
    \item \(\theta\) is the angle between the force and the position vector.
\end{itemize}

The relationship between the direction of the torque
and the direction of the applied force is given by the \textbf{right-hand rule}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the force.
    \item Make a thumbs-up gesture with your right thumb.
    \item Your right thumb points in the direction of the torque.
\end{itemize}

% To-do: add image

\textbf{Newton's second law for rotational movement}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:newtons-2nd-law-rotational}
    \begin{aligned}
        \bm{\tau} &= I \bm{\alpha} \\
        \tau &= I \alpha
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Atwood Machines with a Pulley of Non-Negligible Mass}

An \textbf{Atwood machine} is a pair of masses connected by a rope and hung over a pulley.

Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) be
        the masses on the left and right respectively.
    \item \(M\) be the mass of the pulley.
    \item
        \(F_1\) be the total force exerted on \(m_1\),
        and similarly for \(F_2\).
    \item
        \(F_\mathrm{T1}\) be the force of tension exerted on \(m_1\)
        and similarly for \(F_\mathrm{T2}\).
    \item \(R\) be the radius of the pulley.
    \item \(\alpha\) be the angular acceleration of the pulley.
    \item \(a\) be the linear acceleration that both masses experience.
\end{itemize}

From \eqRef{eq:newtons-2nd-law-rotational},
\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \begin{aligned}
            \tau &= I \alpha \\
            &= I \frac{a}{R}
        \end{aligned} \\
        \begin{aligned}
            F_1 &= F_\mathrm{T1} - m_1 g \\
            F_2 &= F_\mathrm{T2} - m_2 g \\
            F_\mathrm{T1} - m_1 g &= m_1 a \\
            F_\mathrm{T1} &= m_1 a + m_1 g \\
            F_\mathrm{T2} - m_2 g &= m_2 a \\
            F_\mathrm{T2} &= m_2 a + m_2 g \\
        \end{aligned} \\
        \\
        \begin{aligned}
            R F_\mathrm{T2} - R F_\mathrm{T1} &= \frac{1}{2} M R a \\
            F_\mathrm{T2} - F_\mathrm{T1} &= \frac{1}{2} M a \\
            m_2 a + m_2 g - \left( m_1 a + m_1 g \right) &= \frac{1}{2} M a \\
            m_2 g - m_1 g &= \frac{1}{2} M a - m_2 a + m_1 a \\
            \left( m_2 - m_1 \right) g &= \left( \frac{1}{2} M - m_2 + m_1 \right) a \\
        \end{aligned} \\
        \begin{aligned}
            a &= \frac{\left( m_2 - m_1 \right) g}{\frac{1}{2} M - m_2 + m_1} \\
            &= \frac{2 \left( m_2 - m_1 \right) g}{M - 2 \left( m_2 + m_1 \right)}
        \end{aligned}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\section{Rolling}

Objects need static friction to roll.

If there is no static friction, the object simply slides rather than rolling,
which causes no energy loss.
The tangential acceleration is 0,
but the acceleration of the center of mass is non-zero.

If the static friction is insufficient, the object \textbf{rolls with slipping},
which causes energy loss.
The tangential acceleration and the acceleration of the center of mass are not equal.

If the static friction reaches a critical point, it \textbf{rolls without slipping},
which does not cause energy loss.
The tangential acceleration and the acceleration of the center of mass are equal.
\begin{equation}
    a_\mathrm{t} = a_\mathrm{cm}
\end{equation}

Imagine an object rolling down a ramp without slipping.
Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(r\) be the radius of an object rolling down a ramp.
    \item \(\alpha\) be the angular acceleration of the rolling object.
    \item \(a\) be the linear acceleration.
    \item \(m\) be the mass of the rolling object.
    \item \(\theta\) be the angle of the ramp from the horizontal.
    \item \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\) be the static friction of the ramp.
    \item \(F_\mathrm{N}\) be the normal force experienced by the object.
    \item \(F_\mathrm{f}\) be the frictional force experienced by the object.
\end{itemize}

Deriving \(\mu_\mathrm{s}\):
\begin{gather}
    \begin{aligned}
        F_\mathrm{N} &= m g \cos\theta \\
        \therefore F_\mathrm{f} &= \mu_\mathrm{s} m g \cos\theta \\
    \end{aligned} \\
    \nonumber \\
    \begin{aligned} \label{eq:rolling-static-friction}
        m g \sin\theta - F_\mathrm{f} &= m a \\
        m g \sin\theta - \mu_\mathrm{s} m g \cos\theta &= m a \\
        \mu_\mathrm{s} &= - \frac{a}{g \cos\theta} + \tan\theta \\
    \end{aligned} \\
    \nonumber \\
    \begin{aligned} \label{eq:rolling-angular-accleration}
        \tau &= I \alpha \\
        r F_\mathrm{f} &= I \alpha \\
        \alpha &= \frac{r F_\mathrm{f}}{I} \\
        &= \frac{r \mu_\mathrm{s} m g \cos\theta}{I} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{gather}
Plug in \eqRef{eq:rolling-angular-accleration}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned} \label{eq:rolling-accleration}
        a &= r \alpha \\
        &= \frac{r^2 \mu_\mathrm{s} m g \cos\theta}{I} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Plug \cref{eq:rolling-accleration} into \eqRef{eq:rolling-static-friction}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mu_\mathrm{s} &= - \frac{
                            \frac{r^2 \mu_\mathrm{s} m g \cos\theta}{I}
                        }
                        {g \cos\theta}
                        + \tan\theta \\
        &= - \frac{\mu_\mathrm{s} m r^2}{I} + \tan\theta \\
        \mu_\mathrm{s} + \frac{\mu_\mathrm{s} m r^2}{I} &= \tan\theta \\
        \mu_\mathrm{s} \left( 1 + \frac{m r^2}{I} \right) &= \tan\theta \\
        \mu_\mathrm{s} &= \frac{\tan\theta}{1 + \frac{m r^2}{I}} \\
        &= \frac{I \tan\theta}{I + m r^2}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\chapter{Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems}

\noindent Suggested mathematical topics: \nameRef{ch:vectors}

\section{Rotational Kinetic Energy}

Similarly to the formula for linear kinetic energy in \eqRef{eq:linear-kinetic-energy},
the formula for \textbf{rotational kinetic energy} is
\begin{equation}
    E_\mathrm{k} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(E_\mathrm{k}\) is the kinetic energy of the rotating object.
    \item \(I\) is the moment of inertia of the object spinning around its axis of rotation.
    \item \(\omega\) is the angular velocity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rotational Work}

\textbf{Rotational work}:
\begin{equation}
    W = \int \tau(\theta) \odif{\theta}
\end{equation}

Example of rotational work is finding the work done by gravity
as a rod swings from horizontal to vertical.
Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \(r\) be the distance between the axis of rotation
        and the rod's center of mass.
    \item \(m\) be the mass of the rod.
\end{itemize}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        W &= \int_0^\frac{\pi}{2} \frac{1}{2} m g \cos\theta \odif{\theta} \\
        &= \frac{3}{2} g [\sin\theta]_0^\frac{\pi}{2} \\
        &= \frac{3}{2} g
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Angular Momentum}

Unsurprisingly, \textbf{angular momentum} is the rotational analogue to linear momentum.
It is defined by the following equation:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{L} = I \bm{\omega}
\end{equation}

Objects moving in a straight lines also have angular momentum:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \bm{L} &= \bm{r} \times \bm{p} \\
        &= m v r \sin\theta
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Conservation of Angular Momentum}

Similarly to linear momentum, angular momentum must be conserved.

The most famous demonstration of \textbf{conservation of angular momentum}
is a person pulling in their arms while they are spinning.
Pulling in their arms decreases the radius between their arms and center of mass,
reducing moment of inertia.
Since angular momentum must be conserved,
their angular velocity increases to compensate.

\section{Orbits} \label{sec:orbits}

\textbf{Kepler's laws of planetary motion}
describe the motion of planets orbiting around a star.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item
        Planetary orbits are elliptical,
        with the star at one of the ellipse's foci.
    \item \label{itm:keplers-2nd-law}
        The line between the star and an orbiting planet
        sweeps across equal amounts of area in equal amounts of time.
    \item
        \(T^2 \propto r^3\).
        See \nameRef{subsec:orbital-velocity} for the derivation.
\end{enumerate}

In elliptical \textbf{orbits}, both objects orbit around the center of mass,
in this context, called the \textbf{barycenter}.
Angular momentum is conserved.

% To-do: add image

Imagine two bodies orbiting each other,
with one labeled A and the other labeled B.
Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(L_\mathrm{A}\) and \(L_\mathrm{B}\) be the angular momenta of A and B, respectively.
    \item \(m_\mathrm{A}\) and \(m_\mathrm{B}\) be the masses of A and B, respectively.
    \item \(v_\mathrm{A}\) and \(v_\mathrm{B}\) be the velocities of A and B, respectively.
    \item \(r_\mathrm{A}\) and \(r_\mathrm{B}\) be the distances of A and B to their barycenter, respectively.
\end{itemize}
\begin{align}
    L_\mathrm{A} &= L_\mathrm{B} \\
    m_\mathrm{A} v_\mathrm{A} r_\mathrm{A} &= m_\mathrm{B} v_\mathrm{B} r_\mathrm{B}
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        v_\mathrm{A} &= v_\mathrm{B} \frac{m_\mathrm{B} r_\mathrm{B}}{m_\mathrm{A} r_\mathrm{A}} \\
        v_\mathrm{B} &= v_\mathrm{A} \frac{m_\mathrm{A} r_\mathrm{A}}{m_\mathrm{B} r_\mathrm{B}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The above equations suggest that A moves much faster
when A is close to the barycenter,
and similarly for B.

This conclusion is actually a consequence of
\hyperref[itm:keplers-2nd-law]{Kepler's second law of planetary motion}.
Since planetary motion is an ellipse,
for the same amount of time to sweep the same area,
the planet would have to travel faster to compensate for the smaller radius.

Mechanical energy of orbiting bodies:
\begin{gather}
    E = U + E_\mathrm{K} \\
    \begin{aligned}
        U &= - \int F \odif{r} \\
        &= - \int G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \odif{r} \\
        &= -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{gather}

\begin{gather}
    E_\mathrm{K} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 \\
    \begin{aligned}
        a_\mathrm{c} &= \frac{v^2}{r} \\
        \therefore v^2 &= a_\mathrm{c} r \\
    \end{aligned} \\
    E_\mathrm{K} = \frac{1}{2} m a_\mathrm{c} r \\
    m a_\mathrm{c} = F_\mathrm{g} = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \\
    \begin{aligned}
        E_\mathrm{K} &= \frac{1}{2} \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2} r \\
        &= \frac{G m_1 m_2}{2 r} \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{gather}

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        E &= - \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r} + \frac{G m_1 m_2}{2 r} \\
        &= - \frac{G m_1 m_2}{2 r}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Escape Velocities}

Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(M\) be the mass of the larger body, e.g., a planet.
    \item \(m\) be the mass of the escaping body, e.g., a spaceship.
    \item \(r\) the radius of the larger body.
    \item \(v\) be the escape velocity.
\end{itemize}
\(- \frac{G M m}{r} + \frac{1}{2} m v^2\) is the total energy.
To escape, you need both 0 gravitational potential energy and 0 kinetic energy:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        - \frac{G M m}{r} + \frac{1}{2} m v^2 &= 0 \\
        \frac{1}{2} m v^2 &= \frac{G M m}{r} \\
        v &= \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{r}}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\chapter{Oscillations}

\noindent Suggested mathematical topics: \nameRef{ch:vectors}, \nameRef{ch:ellipses}

\section{Simple Harmonic Motion}

\textbf{Periodic motion} is any specific motion that repeats periodically.

\textbf{Periodic oscillation} is a specific type of periodic motion
where an object moves back and forth in a fix location.

\textbf{Simple harmonic motion} is a type of periodic oscillation
where the net force is only a restoring force
that is a function of the object's distance from equilibrium.

Oscillations can be defined by three parameters.
Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \(A\) be the \textbf{amplitude},
        which is half the difference between the maximum and minimum values.
        Its SI units depends on what we are measuring,
        e.g., \(\unit{\metre}\) for distance, \(\unit{\radian}\) for angle,
        \(\unit{\metre \per \second}\) for velocity,
        \(\unit{\ampere}\) for current, etc.
    \item
        \(\omega\) be the \textbf{angular frequency},
        which is a measure of how fast the oscillation cycles
        and has SI units of \(\unit{\radian \per \second}\).
    \item
        \(\phi\) be the \textbf{phase},
        which is the horizontal translation of the cycle,
        and has SI units of \(\unit{\radian}\).
\end{itemize}
Thus, the general form looks like
\begin{equation}
    f(t) = A \cos(\omega + \phi)
\end{equation}

Several other measurements are related to angular frequency.

\textbf{Rotational frequency} or simply \textbf{frequency}
is the number of oscillations per unit of time,
with SI units of hertz (\(\unit{\hertz}\)) or \(\unit{\per \second}\).
The relationship between angular frequency and rotational frequency is
\begin{equation}
    f = \frac{\omega}{2 \pi}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(f\) is the rotational frequency.
    \item \(\omega\) is the angular frequency.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Period} is the time for one complete oscillation,
with SI units of \(\unit{\second}\).
The period \(T\) can be expressed in terms of angular frequency as
\begin{equation}
    T = \frac{2 \pi}{\omega}
\end{equation}

\section{Spring Oscillators}

\textbf{Spring oscillators} are composed of a mass
attached to the end of a spring.

Let
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(x\) be the position of the mass.
    \item \(a\) be the acceleration of the mass.
    \item \(m\) be the mass attached to the spring.
    \item \(k\) be the spring constant of the spring.
    \item \(t\) be the time.
\end{itemize}

Derivation of the position function:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        F &= m a \\
        -k x &= m \odv[2]{x}{t} \\
        \odv[2]{x}{t} &= - \frac{k}{m} x
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We know that the answer when differentiated twice
is is proportional to the negative of itself,
so we can guess the following solution:
\begin{align}
    x(t) &= A \cos(\omega t + \phi) \\
    v(t) = \odv{x}{t} &= - A \omega \sin(\omega t + \phi) \\
    a(t) = \odv{v}{t} = \odv[2]{x}{t} &= - A \omega^2 \cos(\omega t + \phi)
\end{align}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(A\) is the amplitude, which is determined by our initial conditions.
    \item
        \(\omega\) is the angular frequency as given by the equation
        \begin{equation}
            \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
        \end{equation}
    \item \(\phi\) is the phase, which is determined by our initial conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Pendula}

A \textbf{pendulum}
(plural \emph{\textbf{pendulums}} or \emph{\textbf{pendula}})
is a swinging rod, possibly with a mass attached to the end.
A grandfather clock is a good example of a pendulum.

The oscillation of pendula is determined by gravity.

\subsection{Ideal Pendula}

Derivation of the angle function for ideal pendula,
i.e., ones with negligible moment of inertia:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:pendulum-forces}
    \begin{aligned}
        F = m a \\
        - m g \sin\theta = m a \\
        - g \sin\theta = a \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The arc length \(s\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        s = \ell \theta \\
        \therefore a = \ell \odv[2]{\theta}{t}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Plug back into \cref{eq:pendulum-forces}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        - g \sin\theta &= \ell \odv[2]{\theta}{t} \\
        \odv[2]{\theta}{t} + \frac{g}{\ell} \sin\theta &= 0
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{small-angle approximation} tells us that for sufficiently small angles
(\qty{0.24}{\radian} gives less than 1\% error),
\begin{gather}
    \sin\theta \approx \theta \\
    \therefore \odv[2]{\theta}{t} + \frac{g}{\ell} \theta \approx 0
\end{gather}
If we guess like we did for the spring oscillators, we find
\begin{equation}
    \theta(t) = A \cos(\omega t + \phi)
\end{equation}
where \(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}\).

\subsection{Physical Pendula}

\textbf{Physical pendula} have non-negligible moment of inertia.

Let \(r\) be the distance between the axis of rotation and center of mass.
Derivation of angle function for a physical pendulum:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \tau &= I \alpha \\
        &= I \odv[2]{\theta}{t} \\
        - r m g \sin\theta &= I \odv[2]{\theta}{t} \\
        \odv[2]{\theta}{t} + \frac{r m g \sin\theta}{I} &= 0 \\
        \odv[2]{\theta}{t} + \frac{r m g \theta}{I} &\approx 0 \\
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We get the result
\begin{equation}
    \theta(t) = A \cos(\omega t + \phi)
\end{equation}
where \(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{r m g}{I}}\).

\section{Torsion Pendula}

\textbf{Torsion} is a torque caused by twisting an object around its axis of rotation.
In a \textbf{torsion pendulum}, an object is hung on a string,
and the string is twisted,
which creates a restorative force of torsion
that causes the object to rotate in an oscillatory manner.
For a torsion pendulum,
the SI units for the spring constant \(k\) is \(\unit{\newton \per \radian}\).

Skipping the differential equations, the derivation for a torsion pendulum is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \sum \tau &= I \alpha \\
        - \tau &= I \alpha \\
        - k \theta &= I \odv[2]{\theta}{t} \\
        - \frac{k}{I} \theta &= \odv[2]{\theta}{t} \\
        \theta(t) = \theta_\mathrm{max} \cos(\omega t + \phi)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item
        \(\theta_\mathrm{max}\) is the amplitude.
        If the initial angular velocity is 0,
        then \(\theta_\mathrm{max}\) will be the initial angle.
        Elsewise, you will have to solve for \(\theta_\mathrm{max}\)
        using the initial angle and angular velocity.
    \item \(\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{I}}\) is the angular frequency.
    \item
        \(\phi\) is the phase shift.
        If the initial angular velocity is 0, \(\phi = 0\).
        Elsewise, you will have to solve for \(\phi\)
        using the initial angle and angular velocity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Resonance}

When something is oscillating,
and an external force is applied at the same frequency as the oscillation,
the amplitude of the oscillation will increase.
This phenomena is known as \textbf{resonance}.

\chapter{Ellipses} \label{ch:ellipses}

A circle is all the points that are equidistant from a center point.
An \textbf{ellipse} is all the points around two points,
known as the \textbf{foci},
such that the sum of the distances to both foci is constant.

Technically, a circle is just a special type of ellipse
where the two foci are the same point.

Instead of having a single diameter,
the longest diameter of an ellipse is the \textbf{major axis},
and the shortest one is called the \textbf{minor axis}.

Similarly, instead of having a single radius,
an ellipse has a \textbf{semi-major axis}, i.e., half the major axis,
and a \textbf{semi-minor axis}, i.e., half the minor axis.
