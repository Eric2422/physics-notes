\chapter{Magnetic Fields and Electromagnetism}

\section{Magnetic fields}

Magnetic fields have a \textbf{north pole} and \textbf{south pole};
by convention, magnetic fields travel from the north pole to the south pole.

Magnetism is caused by the movement and spin of charged particles.
Thus, spinning electrons create a magnetic field around themselves.

The direction of magnetic fields producted by spinning \emph{positive} charges
follow the \emph{right}-hand rule [\pgRef{sec:direction-of-rotation}],
with the particle spinning in the direction of your right fingers
and the \emph{north} pole in the directions of your right thumb.

However, the direction for \emph{negative} charges is reversed.
If you use the right-hand rule,
your right thumb will instead point in the direction of the \emph{south} pole.
If you use the \emph{left}-hand rule,
your left thumb will point in the direction of the \emph{north} pole.
Use whichever method suits you better,
but I prefer the left-hand rule to keep them separate.

\section{Magnetic Field Diagrams}

Magnetic field lines are drawn from north to south.

\section{Gauss's Law for Magnetism}

In addition to his law for electricity, Gauss also had a law for magnetism.
\textbf{Gauss's law for magnetism} states
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gausss-law-magnetism}
    \oiint_S \vecb{B} \cdot \odif{\vecb{A}} = 0
\end{equation}
That is to say, magnetic fields must form closed loops.
As a consequence, all magnets must form \textbf{magnetic dipoles},
with both a north and south pole.
\textbf{Magnetic monopoles},
where a magnet has a only a single north or south pole,
violate Gauss's law for magnetism.

Currently physicists are attempting to discover magnetic monopoles.
If successful,
we would need to replace Gauss's law for magnetism with a more complete one.

\section{Ampère's Law}

Electric currents generate magnetic fields that coil around themselves.
Ignoring edge effects, for a sufficiently long wire with constant current,
the magnetic field is given by \textbf{Ampere's law}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:amperes-law}
    \oint_C \vecb{B} \cdot \odif{\vecb{\ell}} = \mu_0 I,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item \(B\) is the magnitude of the magnetic field.
    \item \(I\) is the current.
    \item \(\mu_0\) is the \textbf{permittivity of free space} or
        \textbf{vacuum permittivity}.
    \item \(r\) is the distance from the wire.
\end{itemize}

\section{Biot-Savart Law}

\textbf{Biot-Savart's law} is a generalization of Ampère's law
to wires that are not sufficiently long
and points where edge effects become non-negligible.
However, it is still subject to the restriction of a constant current.
It states
\begin{equation}
    B = \int \frac{\mu_0 I \odif{\ell} \times \vecb{r}}{4 \pi \vecb{r}^2}
\end{equation}

\section{Solenoids}

\textbf{Solenoids} are coils of wire that generate a straight magnetic field
in their center.
The magnetic field \(B\) generated by an empty solenoid is given by
\begin{equation}
    B = \mu_0 I \frac{N}{\ell}
\end{equation}
where \(N\) is the number of turns in the wire
and \(\ell\) is the length of the solenoid,
\emph{not} the wire in the solenoid.
